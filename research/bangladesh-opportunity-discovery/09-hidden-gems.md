# Step 7 — Ten Hidden Gems

**Selection rule (deliberately different from the ranking):** small-to-medium initial investment **+** low current competition **+** strong international evidence **+** a large, verified Bangladesh gap **+** a credible path to rapid scale. These are *not* the biggest markets. Several sit in Tier B or C of the main ranking. What they share is an unusually favourable ratio of **evidence and impact to capital and competition** — and a reason they have been overlooked.

---

## Gem 1 — Formal used lead-acid battery recycling (#49)
**Capital:** Tk 40–100 crore for a compliant plant; **Tk 3–8 crore** to start as a collection business.
**Why it's a gem:** the evidence base on the problem is extraordinary and almost nobody is acting on it. **~28.5 million Bangladeshi children with blood lead above 5 µg/dL; ~20.6 million IQ points lost among under-fives; an estimated USD 10.9bn ≈ 3.6% of GDP; 1,100+ informal ULAB operations.** [VERIFIED] A commodity with an established world price is being produced by poisoning children, and the formal alternative is a normal industrial business.
**Why it's overlooked:** lead poisoning is invisible. It produces no acute event, no headline, no identifiable victim — a child who loses four IQ points never knows. There is no constituency, so there is no policy, so there is no market. The opportunity exists *because* of the absence of outrage.
**What unlocks it:** a battery EPR levy. Without it the informal smelter always wins on price; with it, formal recycling is straightforwardly profitable.

## Gem 2 — Faecal sludge management as a scheduled utility (#15)
**Capital:** Tk 10–22 crore per city including treatment.
**Why it's a gem:** each secondary city is a **defensible local monopoly** with a recurring tariff, the model is proven in Malaysia (three decades), India (national policy) and Senegal, and Bangladesh already has the pilots and the policy framework. Roughly **17 million urban households**, most non-sewered [CALCULATED].
**Why it's overlooked:** it is faecal sludge. Investors do not go to conferences about it, the unit economics look small city-by-city, and it carries occupational stigma. But it is a utility business with contracted revenue and no competition.
**What unlocks it:** billing the sanitation charge on the water bill, which is a municipal ordinance, not a technology.

## Gem 3 — Open contracting analytics on e-GP (#6)
**Capital:** Tk 3–7 crore. The lowest-capital opportunity in the dossier.
**Why it's a gem:** Bangladesh has run e-GP since **2011** and the data exists. Adoption already cuts contract price-to-cost by **10.25–11.85%**, and universal coverage could save up to **USD 1.76bn/yr**. [VERIFIED] A bid-intelligence subscription for contractors is a revenue business from month one, and it generates the dataset that makes the anti-collusion product possible.
**Why it's overlooked:** it reads as an anti-corruption NGO project, so commercial investors ignore it and donors fund it as advocacy. Nobody has framed it as a **B2B data business with a governance side-effect** — which is what ProZorro's DOZORRO layer actually is.
**What unlocks it:** publishing e-GP data in an open standard. One administrative decision.

## Gem 4 — Childcare as labour-market infrastructure (#34)
**Capital:** Tk 4–10 crore for a first cluster.
**Why it's a gem:** the causal chain is unusually tight and unusually well-evidenced *in Bangladesh*: **73% of the unemployed female labour force exits after childbirth**, and female participation fell from **25.3m to 23.7m women between 2023 and 2024**. [VERIFIED] Employers already bear the cost as turnover. The service can therefore be sold on a hard retention ROI rather than on social value.
**Why it's overlooked:** it is categorised as a welfare obligation under the Labour Act rather than as a service business, so it attracts token compliance rather than investment. Nobody has built the retention business case.
**What unlocks it:** permitting shared, off-site certified centres to discharge the statutory employer obligation.

## Gem 5 — Online dispute resolution for lenders (#3)
**Capital:** Tk 6–12 crore. No legislation required to start.
**Why it's a gem:** **4,639,476 pending cases**; High Court judges carrying **~6,463 cases each** and clearing ~435 a year. [VERIFIED] Meanwhile MFIs and MFS lenders write off recoverable balances because litigation is uneconomic. A per-resolved-case fee paid by the institution is a clean B2B model, and India's ODR sector proves it works commercially.
**Why it's overlooked:** justice is assumed to be a government problem. But the paying customer here is a lender, not a court — and lenders are already spending the money inefficiently.
**What unlocks it:** enforceable mediation clauses in lending contracts, which existing law already permits.

## Gem 6 — Crate pooling for horticulture (a sub-component of #25)
**Capital:** Tk 2–5 crore for a crate pool. The single smallest entry in this dossier.
**Why it's a gem:** a meaningful share of Bangladesh's **20–44% post-harvest loss** and **USD 2.4bn annual loss** is simple mechanical damage from jute sacks and overloading. [VERIFIED] Reusable plastic crates with a deposit-and-rental model are a proven, boring, immediately profitable asset-rental business that reduces national food loss as a side-effect.
**Why it's overlooked:** it is too unglamorous to be a startup and too commercial to be a donor project. It falls between the two funding worlds entirely.
**What unlocks it:** nothing. It can start next month.

## Gem 7 — Community lay-counsellor mental health for RMG employers (#39)
**Capital:** Tk 3–8 crore.
**Why it's a gem:** this has arguably the **strongest causal evidence of any opportunity here** — randomised trials published in *JAMA* (Friendship Bench, Zimbabwe) and *The Lancet* (Sangath, India), formalised by WHO in mhGAP and PM+. [VERIFIED] And Bangladesh has a trained non-specialist psychosocial workforce left over from the Rohingya response that has never been redeployed. The buyer is an RMG employer with a measurable absenteeism and turnover problem.
**Why it's overlooked:** stigma, plus the assumption that mental health is a public-health charity rather than an employer service line.
**What unlocks it:** one RMG group willing to run a measured pilot, and a brand willing to co-fund it.

## Gem 8 — Land due-diligence and title search as a product (a sub-component of #2)
**Capital:** Tk 1–3 crore.
**Why it's a gem:** **~80% of rural court cases are land disputes** and **>4 million land cases are pending**. [VERIFIED] Every property transaction in Bangladesh already involves paying someone — informally and opaquely — to check the record. Productising that as a paid, standardised due-diligence report is a business today, requires no legal reform, and quietly accumulates the proprietary dataset that makes title insurance possible later.
**Why it's overlooked:** it looks like a lawyer's job rather than a company. Nobody has industrialised it.
**What unlocks it:** nothing. Digitised records already permit it.

## Gem 9 — Traceable jhut aggregation (a sub-component of #50)
**Capital:** Tk 5–15 crore for aggregation and sorting; no recycling plant needed.
**Why it's a gem:** **~400,000 tonnes/yr** of clean, single-composition, geographically concentrated post-industrial textile waste; **~USD 3bn** market potential; **USD 500–700m/yr** of possible import saving. [VERIFIED] And the actual bottleneck is not recycling capacity — it is **traceable feedstock**, which is an aggregation and documentation problem, not a capital-equipment problem.
**Why it's overlooked:** everyone looks at the expensive end (recycling plants, and the cautionary example of Renewcell's 2024 bankruptcy) and ignores the cheap end where the constraint actually sits.
**What unlocks it:** bonded-warehouse and customs rules that currently make it easier to export production waste than to recycle it domestically.

## Gem 10 — Battery-as-a-service for electric three-wheelers (a sub-component of #43 × #49)
**Capital:** Tk 10–25 crore for a district-level swap network.
**Why it's a gem:** it solves three problems with one asset. It makes vehicle finance viable by removing the largest cost from the driver's instalment; it captures **100% of the battery stream**, which is the only reliable way to keep lead out of informal smelters; and it creates recurring revenue with a physical local moat. M-KOPA's Kenyan precedent — **USD 1.6bn disbursed, 4.8m customers, 5,000+ electric motorbikes financed** — proves the financing half. [VERIFIED]
**Why it's overlooked:** electric three-wheelers occupy a legal grey zone in Bangladesh and are periodically subject to bans, so formal capital stays away — leaving a multi-million-vehicle market to informal rental owners and informal battery recyclers.
**What unlocks it:** legal recognition and a safety standard for electric three-wheelers, which is the single highest-value regulatory fix available to Bangladesh's informal transport economy.

## The common pattern

Eight of these ten are overlooked for the same structural reason: **they sit in the gap between what investors classify as a business and what donors classify as a cause.** Lead poisoning, faecal sludge, procurement integrity, childcare, mental health and dispute resolution are all treated as social problems with charitable solutions — so they receive grants, run as pilots, and never scale. Each one, restructured around an institutional payer who already bears the cost (a producer paying EPR, a household paying a sanitation tariff, a contractor paying for bid intelligence, a factory paying for retention, a lender paying for recovery), becomes an ordinary business with a durable moat.

**That gap — between the charitable framing and the commercial one — is where Bangladesh's most asymmetric opportunities are hiding.**
