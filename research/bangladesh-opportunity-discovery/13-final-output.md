## B. Top 10 Opportunities Bangladesh Should Seriously Pursue

This list is **not** the top ten of the ranking. It is the ten that a national strategy should pursue, which weights *national consequence* over private return and deliberately includes several hard, low-feasibility items.

### B1. Mandatory VAT e-invoicing with a revenue-analytics layer (#4)
Bangladesh's tax/GDP ratio **fell to 6.56% in FY25** and NBR missed target by **Tk 92,625 crore**. [VERIFIED] Nothing else on this list is affordable until this changes. Each percentage point of tax/GDP is roughly **USD 5bn/yr** [CALCULATED]. Brazil has run mandatory e-invoicing since 2008 and India since 2020, progressively lowering thresholds — the extension itself is the evidence it works. The politically survivable framing is not enforcement but **credit**: a compliant firm's verified invoice history becomes shareable to lenders, which is what makes e-invoicing a benefit rather than a burden. **Pursue first, because everything else depends on it.**

### B2. Government-to-government zero-fee migration corridors (#7)
A Bangladeshi worker spends **Tk 416,789** to earn **Tk 23,693/month** — **17+ months of work to repay the cost of getting the work**. [VERIFIED] Korea's EPS cut recruitment costs from **USD 3,509 (2001) to USD 941 (2014)** by removing private intermediaries through government-to-government arrangements. [VERIFIED] Applying that to 200,000 Bangladeshi workers a year returns roughly **Tk 70bn annually to the poorest migrating households** [CALCULATED] — with no new industry required, only the replacement of an intermediary chain. The post-August-2024 collapse in hundi usage demonstrated that this system's political economy *can* shift. That window will not stay open.

### B3. A strategic health purchasing agency, built on SSK (#8)
Out-of-pocket health spending reached **~79% in 2024**; the poorest households spend **~35% of income** on health. [VERIFIED] Bangladesh's own SSK scheme already demonstrated **92% lower out-of-pocket expenditure and 72% lower catastrophic health expenditure** among inpatient users — in Bangladesh, on Bangladeshi patients — and covers 400,000 people in three upazilas. [VERIFIED] Covering 10 million poorest households at SSK's own **Tk 1,000 premium costs Tk 10bn/yr** [CALCULATED]. The single change required to make it scalable is **empanelling private providers**, which India's PM-JAY proved at a scale of **127 million hospital admissions**. [VERIFIED] This is the clearest "finish what you started" item in the dossier.

### B4. Conclusive land titling with a state indemnity fund (#2)
**~80% of rural court cases** are land disputes and **>4 million land cases** are pending. [VERIFIED] Digitisation began in 2010 and has automated an unreliable record rather than made it reliable, because a khatian still records possession, not title. The reform that matters is legal, not technological: make the register conclusive, back it with an indemnity fund financed by a registration levy, and declare it district by district after a publicised objection window. The by-products are a bankable collateral class and relief for the largest single category of pending litigation.

### B5. Industrial rooftop and floating solar under RESCO contracts (#17)
Bangladesh has **~5,000 MW of identified industrial rooftop potential** and **~116 MW installed under net metering**. [VERIFIED] The **Net Metering Guidelines 2025** and the **December 2025 mandatory-rooftop circular** removed the regulatory barrier within the last year. The remaining barrier is capex on factory balance sheets, which the RESCO model eliminates entirely. India's PM-KUSUM demonstrated that a South Asian solar market can scale **4.2× to 25× in a single year** once financing and offtake are resolved. [VERIFIED] This is the **easiest** large opportunity in the dossier and the timing is exceptional.

### B6. Global capability centres and a real IT services industry (#48)
The Philippines built **USD 38bn of revenue and 1.82 million jobs — about 8% of GDP** — from an economy with similar structural characteristics. [VERIFIED] Bangladesh exports **USD 724.6m** against a **USD 5bn** target, with **500,000 active freelancers earning USD 500–700/month**. [VERIFIED] The gap is not talent; it is that no global CFO has approved a Bangladesh centre, so none will. The strategy is therefore narrow and specific: **land two or three anchor tenants, publish their results, and make a binding no-shutdown connectivity commitment.** Services face no tariffs — which makes this the single best answer to LDC graduation.

### B7. Formal battery and e-waste recycling under an EPR levy (#49)
**~28.5 million children** with blood lead above 5 µg/dL; **~20.6 million IQ points** lost among under-fives; an estimated **USD 10.9bn ≈ 3.6% of GDP**; **1,100+ informal ULAB operations**; **97% of e-waste handled informally**. [VERIFIED] The **Hazardous Waste (e-waste) Management Rules 2021** exist and have stalled, with TIB's critique providing an explicit fix list. The intervention is not enforcement against a thousand small smelters — it is an **EPR levy that lets formal collectors outbid them**. The cognitive damage is permanent and compounds across the future workforce, which makes delay uniquely costly.

### B8. Foundational literacy and numeracy at scale (#29)
Learning poverty of **51–57%** with **~98% enrolment** [VERIFIED] means Bangladesh solved access and did not solve learning. TaRL is among the most-replicated randomised results in development economics, and Bangladesh has in BRAC the delivery capability most countries attempting it lack. National coverage costs **Tk 7.2–18bn/yr** [CALCULATED] — small relative to almost everything else on this list. The pivotal permission is trivially small: allow children to be grouped **by level** for a defined daily period.

### B9. Common effluent treatment as a performance-contracted utility (#20)
Leather is stuck at **USD 1bn against USD 5bn potential**, and **300 of the 1,710 points** required for LWG certification relate to the CETP that "continues to release untreated water". [VERIFIED] Only **~8 Bangladeshi institutions** hold LWG certification, so tanneries sell raw leather to China at a discount. Very few opportunities anywhere have a bottleneck this narrow standing in front of a prize this large. The fix is a contracting reform — **pay per m³ treated to standard, with published continuous online monitoring** — not another capital project.

### B10. Bus route franchising for Dhaka and Chattogram (#11)
Congestion costs somewhere between **Tk 370bn/yr and USD 3–5bn/yr**, with **~5 million work hours** lost annually; and Bangladesh has **South Asia's highest road fatality rate at ~102 deaths per 10,000 vehicles** against India's 13. [VERIFIED] Both problems share one cause: drivers paid per passenger racing each other on unregulated routes. Bogotá delivered a **32% travel-time reduction** at **USD 5.4–5.9m/km** — one to two orders of magnitude below metro cost. [VERIFIED] The pivotal change is amending route-permit law to allow **company-only permits**. It is the hardest item here politically and among the highest-return.

**The pattern OceanBlu should notice.** Eight of these ten sell to an institution that **already bears the cost being addressed** — a factory paying for electricity, a distributor financing dealers informally, a retailer absorbing spoilage, a fleet owner replacing batteries, an employer paying for turnover. None requires creating new demand. In a market where consumers are extremely price-sensitive and institutions are cost-blind, **the fastest route to revenue is to find a cost someone is already paying and charge less for it.**

**And the thing OceanBlu should build that nobody else will:** every one of these businesses generates a proprietary verification dataset — repayment behaviour, lab quality scores, title search history, tender award prices, waste chain-of-custody, placement outcomes. In a country whose core deficit is that **nothing can be verified**, those datasets are the only durable moats available. Build the operating business; the data asset is the actual prize.

## G. Final Strategic Conclusion

> **"If Bangladesh wanted to become significantly more productive, competitive, digitally advanced and prosperous over the next 10 years, which 10 systems, industries or business models should it seriously consider importing, adapting and building?"**

### The thesis: Bangladesh's binding constraint is not capital, labour, or electricity. It is that nothing can be verified.

Bangladesh built a world-class *volume* economy — the world's second-largest apparel exporter, USD 32.8bn of remittance, 239 million mobile money accounts, near-universal school enrolment, near-universal improved water access — on top of institutions that cannot verify anything. That single deficit explains an implausibly wide range of apparently unrelated problems:

- **4.64 million court cases**, of which land disputes are ~80% of the rural docket, because a land record proves possession rather than title.
- A **USD 2.8bn MSME credit gap** with only 28% of SMEs accessing formal credit, in a country with 239 million mobile money accounts — because a lender can verify land but not revenue.
- **Insurance penetration of 0.33% of GDP** in one of the most disaster-exposed countries on earth, because losses cannot be assessed cheaply and claims payment cannot be trusted.
- Leather **stuck at USD 1bn against USD 5bn potential**, because 300 certification points of effluent compliance cannot be evidenced.
- Migrants paying **USD 3,900 to earn USD 200 a month**, and being classified as unskilled after a decade abroad, because no skill can be proved.
- **USD 5bn a year leaving the country for foreign hospitals**, because no Bangladeshi hospital publishes an outcome.
- Shrimp at **400 kg/ha against India's 5,500**, partly because competitors adopted traceability and Bangladesh did not.
- **A tax/GDP ratio of 6.56%**, the world's lowest tier, because transactions leave no verifiable trail.

Every one of those is the same failure wearing different clothes. And critically: **each has a working, financially self-sustaining precedent in a country poorer than, or only modestly richer than, Bangladesh.** Rwanda verifies land and government services. India verifies invoices, identities and financial consent. Korea verifies migrant recruitment. Kenya verifies repayment behaviour. Estonia verifies everything and saves 2% of GDP doing it.

**The highest-return things Bangladesh can import over 2026–2035 are not products or factories. They are verification systems.**

### The ten systems to import, adapt and build

1. **A transaction verification system** — mandatory VAT e-invoicing (Brazil, India). It funds everything else and simultaneously creates the best SME underwriting data the country will ever have.
2. **A property verification system** — conclusive land title with a state indemnity fund (Rwanda, Estonia). It empties the largest category of the court docket and creates a bankable collateral class.
3. **A data-movement system** — a national interoperability layer with consent (Estonia's X-Road, Rwanda's Irembo). At least a dozen of these fifty opportunities become cheaper the day it exists.
4. **A skills verification system** — G2G migration corridors plus a skills passport and RPL (Korea's EPS, the Philippines' TESDA). It converts a million departures a year from a cost-led trade into a skill-led one.
5. **A health purchasing system** — a strategic purchaser buying defined packages from accredited public and private providers (Thailand, India, and **Bangladesh's own SSK, which already proved a 92% out-of-pocket reduction and was never scaled**).
6. **A revenue verification system for credit** — cash-flow-based SME lending on consent-shared financial data (India's Account Aggregator and OCEN), riding the payment interoperability that launched in November 2025.
7. **A repayment verification system for the informal economy** — PAYGO asset financing (Kenya's M-KOPA), which builds credit files for tens of millions of people who currently have none, starting with electric three-wheelers.
8. **A quality verification system for industry and food** — accredited laboratories, published external quality scores, effluent monitoring published continuously, and chain-of-custody traceability (Italy's tannery consortium, India's Grapenet, the global lab accreditation regime). This is what unlocks leather, shrimp, horticulture and diagnostics simultaneously.
9. **A learning verification system** — measure what children can actually do and group them by level, then publish provider outcomes and let funding follow the learner (Pratham's TaRL, Singapore's SkillsFuture, Indonesia's Kartu Prakerja — including its honest rural failure).
10. **A services export industry that requires no border verification at all** — global capability centres and IT-BPM (the Philippines' USD 38bn, 1.82 million jobs). This is the hedge: it is the one growth engine that LDC graduation cannot touch, because services face no tariffs.

### Why the sequencing matters more than the list

The temptation is to start with the visible items — buses, cities, hospitals, ports. That would be a mistake. The correct order is:

1. **Rules first (Year 1, near-zero cost).** An EPR levy on batteries. A performance contract for the Savar CETP. Publishing e-GP data in an open standard. Recognising off-site certified childcare. Legalising and standardising electric three-wheelers. These are six rule changes that cost the Treasury almost nothing and could unlock more private investment than the entire public capital programme.
2. **Fiscal and data foundation (Years 1–3).** E-invoicing and the data exchange layer. Without revenue and without data that moves, everything below is unaffordable or unbuildable.
3. **Institutions (Years 2–5).** Land title, health purchasing, migration corridors, foundational literacy. All legislation-dependent, all slow, all worth starting now so the evidence exists when the money does.
4. **Capital programmes (Years 3–8).** Bus franchising, transit finance, secondary cities. Hardest politically, and they depend on the revenue base and the institutional capability built above.

### The deadline nobody can move

**24 November 2026.** LDC graduation removes the preference regime that up to **USD 8bn/yr of exports** depends on, in a sector employing ~4 million people, most of them women. [VERIFIED] Bangladesh cannot restore the preferences. It can do three things instead, and all three are in this dossier: **make its existing exports compliant** (leather's CETP, shrimp's traceability, textiles' circularity), **build a services export column that tariffs cannot touch** (GCCs, care workforce, IT), and **raise the domestic productivity** that has to substitute for the lost preference margin (energy cost, logistics cost, skills, credit).

### The uncomfortable conclusion

Most of what Bangladesh needs over the next decade does not need to be invented, and much of it does not even need to be imported — **it needs to be finished**. SSK proved health protection works and covers 400,000 people. e-GP has cut contract costs by 10–12% since 2011 and its data has never been analysed. Milk Vita has been an Anand-pattern cooperative for fifty years. Community clinics reach 14,000 villages. The Accord/RSC rebuilt factory safety across 1,600 factories and was never productised. IDCOL delivered one of the world's largest solar programmes. Bangladesh's most reliable pattern is not failure to innovate — it is **failure to scale its own successful pilots**.

That is a more hopeful diagnosis than it sounds. It means the binding constraint is not capability or evidence. It is the willingness to take something that already works on 400,000 people and run it on 40 million.
