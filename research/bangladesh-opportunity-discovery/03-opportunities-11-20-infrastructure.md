## Opportunity #11 — Bus route franchising and BRT for Dhaka and Chattogram

### 1. The Core Idea
Replace Dhaka's owner-operator bus free-for-all with **route franchising**: the city tenders exclusive route bundles to companies that must run scheduled service on gross-cost contracts, with fares collected by the authority and drivers on salary rather than daily takings. Add segregated bus priority on the highest-demand corridors. The reform is institutional; the concrete is secondary.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 17, 18, 101. Dhaka congestion costs are estimated between **Tk 370bn/yr** and **USD 3–5bn/yr** depending on methodology, with ~**5 million work hours** lost annually [VERIFIED — wide range; treat as order of magnitude]. Bangladesh has **South Asia's highest road fatality rate at ~102 deaths per 10,000 vehicles** (vs India 13, Sri Lanka 7), and crash costs are estimated at up to **5.1% of GDP**. Both problems share one cause: drivers paid per passenger race each other on unregulated routes. [VERIFIED]

### 3. International Benchmark
- **Colombia — TransMilenio, Bogotá** (2000): BRT with route franchising and fare-box separation.
- **Colombia — Medellín Metrocable** (2004) for topographically hard access.
- **Chile — Transantiago/RED**, and **Lagos BRT-Lite** (2008) as the low-cost variant.
- **Dhaka — "Dhaka Nagar Poribohon"** franchising pilot (domestic precedent).

### 4. Proof That It Works
TransMilenio Phase I cost roughly **USD 5.4–5.9m per km** (Phase II ~USD 13.3m/km) — one to two orders of magnitude below metro cost — and delivered a **32% reduction in average travel time for BRT passengers**, a **44% reduction in sulphur dioxide**, **15–20% property value uplift within 1 km of stations**, and a **~9% shift of car owners to public transport**. [VERIFIED] Medellín's first Metrocable line cost ~**USD 24m** and the second ~**USD 47m**, and is documented as having improved access to education and jobs in hillside settlements. [VERIFIED]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Early-stage / Fragmented (B/C).** Bangladesh has a partially built BRT corridor (Gazipur–Airport) with a long and troubled delivery history, a metro line (MRT-6) in operation, and a limited "Dhaka Nagar Poribohon" franchising experiment. What does not exist is **city-wide route rationalisation with company-based operation and fare-box separation** — the part that actually produces the benefits. [ASSUMPTION on current franchising coverage; VERIFIED that the franchising concept has been piloted.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Dhaka's density is among the highest on earth, which makes bus economics unusually good: high boardings per bus-km, short headways, and strong farebox recovery. The reform is also cheap — franchising a route bundle requires contracts and enforcement, not tunnels. And because MRT lines are now arriving, bus feeder rationalisation is the difference between a metro that carries 300,000/day and one that carries far more.

### 7. Localization
- **Buy out or convert existing permit holders into shareholders of the franchise company**, as Bogotá and Lagos both had to do. Attempting to expropriate permit holders is how this reform dies in Dhaka.
- **Start with gross-cost contracts on 3–5 route bundles**, not a city-wide big bang.
- **Salaried drivers with off-street terminals** — the single change that most reduces racing-related crashes.
- **Fare integration with the metro and with MFS/Bangla QR** rather than a bespoke card, exploiting Bangladesh's existing payment rails.
- **Do not lead with expensive segregated infrastructure.** Bus-priority lanes with camera enforcement deliver most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost, and can be delivered inside one political cycle.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**PPP.** Dhaka Transport Coordination Authority (DTCA) and the two city corporations as contracting authority; private bus operating companies (formed by consolidating existing owners) as franchisees; a fare-collection and control-centre operator.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Gross-cost franchise payments** from the authority to operators, funded by farebox plus advertising, plus — critically — **land value capture** (Opp 12) along corridors. Private revenue: operating companies, fare system operator, depot and terminal concessions, advertising.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** passengers (fares); city/state (subsidy gap); advertisers; landowners via value capture.
- **Uses:** ~20m Dhaka residents and Chattogram commuters.
- **Benefits:** commuters, employers (labour-market access), and the health system (crashes and air pollution).

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** if Dhaka's congestion cost is Tk 370bn–USD 5bn/yr, even recovering 10% is Tk 37bn–USD 500m/yr of value.
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** the farebox. At a conservative 8m daily bus trips at Tk 25 average fare → **Tk 200m/day ≈ Tk 73bn/yr (USD 600m/yr)** of fare revenue that currently flows through an informal, untaxed, uncontracted system. Capturing that into contracted operations is the business.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** one franchise bundle of 300–500 buses → Tk 3–8bn/yr of contracted revenue.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
Travel-time savings (Bogotá's 32% is the benchmark), road deaths (the largest single addressable public-health win in transport), air quality (which costs ~8.3% of GDP in health terms), and female labour force participation — safety and reliability of transport is a documented constraint on women's work travel.

### 13. Technology Required
AVL/GPS fleet tracking, control centre, automated fare collection integrated with Bangla QR/MFS, camera enforcement of bus lanes, and a scheduling/planning system. All commodity technology.

### 14. Government Role
- **DTCA**, Dhaka North and South City Corporations, Chattogram City Corporation, BRTA, Ministry of Road Transport and Bridges.
- **Government:** legislate route franchising, extinguish or convert individual route permits, contract and enforce.
- **Private:** operate buses, run the fare system, build depots.
- **PPP:** essential.
- **Policy change:** amendment to route-permit law to allow company-only permits — the pivotal legal change.

### 15. Major Barriers
**Political economy of permit holders and transport unions** — the dominant barrier, and the reason previous attempts stalled. Also: enforcement of bus lanes against private cars; fare-level politics; and the credibility of the contracting authority.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**9/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**9/10.**

### 18. Why Now?
MRT-6 is operating and further lines are under construction; feeder rationalisation is now urgent or the metro underperforms. Congestion has become a first-order political issue, and the post-2024 period has weakened some of the entrenched permit-holding interests.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** one high-demand corridor bundle (e.g. Mirpur–Motijheel) plus one Chattogram corridor.
- **Users:** 150,000–300,000 daily passengers.
- **Partners:** DTCA, city corporations, existing bus owner associations, an MFS provider, a fleet-tech vendor.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** Tk 150–400 crore including buses (or Tk 20–40 crore if operators finance vehicles).
- **KPIs:** headway adherence; average speed; crashes per million bus-km; farebox recovery ratio; passenger satisfaction.
- **Success:** contracted service running at ≥85% schedule adherence with fare revenue captured centrally, and a crash rate materially below the corridor baseline.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
A startup cannot win the franchise; it can win the **layer around it**. Build first: **fleet telematics, driver-behaviour scoring and fare-collection software for existing bus owners** — sold today on the promise of reducing fuel theft and fare leakage, which are the owners' actual pain points. First customers: the larger bus companies and the Nagar Poribohon operators. Moat: operational data and integration with the payment rails. Scale: telematics → fare system operator → control-centre concession when franchising arrives.

## Opportunity #12 — Land value capture and rail-plus-property financing

### 1. The Core Idea
Fund mass transit from the land value it creates. Two instruments: (a) **rail-plus-property** — grant the transit authority development rights over station sites and depots, as Hong Kong's MTR does; (b) **betterment levy / TOD zoning** — allow higher floor-area ratios near stations in exchange for a payment into the transit fund.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 19, 94. Bangladesh is building metro lines and expressways almost entirely on sovereign debt and concessional loans, while private landowners near stations capture the value uplift for free. Bogotá's evidence shows that uplift is real and large: **15–20% property value increase within 1 km of BRT stations**. [VERIFIED] Meanwhile local government has almost no own-source revenue (gap 13).

### 3. International Benchmark
- **Hong Kong — MTR Corporation "Rail + Property"** model (since the 1970s–80s): the transit operator is also the station-area developer, and property profits fund rail.
- **Japan — private railway companies** (Tokyu, Hankyu) with integrated rail-retail-housing development.
- **India — Delhi Metro property development; Hyderabad and Ahmedabad TOD zoning**.
- **Colombia — Bogotá's betterment levy (contribución de valorización)**, a long-standing municipal instrument.

### 4. Proof That It Works
MTR is the world's reference case of a *profitable* urban rail operator, and its profitability is explicitly attributable to property development rights rather than fares — the model has been studied and partially replicated in Shenzhen, Delhi and London. [VERIFIED that the MTR R+P model exists and is the standard citation for self-financing transit; specific profit-share figures should be taken from MTR's annual report before quoting.] Bogotá's measured 15–20% station-proximity value uplift quantifies the capturable base in a middle-income context. [VERIFIED]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Absent (A).** Bangladesh has no land value capture instrument in operation, no TOD zoning framework tied to transit funding, and DMTCL does not hold development rights as a financing mechanism. [ASSUMPTION — verify with RAJUK and DMTCL; the absence is strongly implied by the debt-financed structure of MRT projects.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Dhaka has extreme land scarcity, extreme density, and a metro network under construction — the exact conditions under which LVC generates the most money. Station-area land in Dhaka is among the most valuable real estate in South Asia relative to income. Capturing even a modest share of uplift on 20+ stations would be transformative for the transit balance sheet, and would also give RAJUK a reason to permit the density Dhaka desperately needs.

### 7. Localization
- **Start with land the state already owns** — depots, station plots, and government land near stations. This avoids the acquisition fight entirely and is the fastest route to a demonstrable success.
- **Sell FAR, don't tax it.** A betterment *levy* on existing owners is politically explosive in Bangladesh; selling *additional* development rights to willing buyers is not. This is the crucial localisation.
- **Ring-fence the proceeds** into a transit fund with published accounts, or the money is absorbed into general revenue and the instrument loses political support.
- **Pair with affordable-housing obligations** on station-area development, or TOD will simply produce luxury towers and intensify displacement.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**Government / PPP.** RAJUK (planning), DMTCL (transit), Ministry of Housing and Public Works, with private developers as concessionaires and a development-finance institution structuring the fund.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Development rights sale + joint-venture development + long lease income.** For private firms: station-area development concessions, mixed-use retail management, and the professional services around it (valuation, transaction structuring).

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** developers buying rights; ultimately property buyers.
- **Uses:** transit authority (as revenue), developers, residents.
- **Benefits:** transit users (funded expansion), the Treasury (less debt), and the city (density where transit exists rather than sprawl).

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [ASSUMPTION]:** Dhaka station-area land values are not published in a usable series — flagged as the key data gap for this opportunity.
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** if 20 stations each support development rights worth Tk 200–600 crore, the one-time capture pool is **Tk 4,000–12,000 crore (USD 330m–1bn)**, plus recurring lease income.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** a first tranche of 3–5 station sites → Tk 600–2,500 crore.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
Fiscal (reduced sovereign borrowing for transit), urban form (density near transit instead of sprawl into flood plains), and — if affordable-housing obligations are attached — a supply response to gap 31.

### 13. Technology Required
GIS-based land and property valuation, cadastral integration (Opp 2), and a property register (Opp 5). Technology is a supporting actor; the instrument is legal and financial.

### 14. Government Role
- **RAJUK**, DMTCL, Ministry of Housing and Public Works, Local Government Division, Ministry of Finance.
- **Government:** create the legal instrument for transferable development rights and premium FAR; assign development rights to the transit entity; ring-fence proceeds.
- **Private:** develop, finance, operate.
- **PPP:** yes.
- **Policy change:** amendments to building rules and the town planning framework to allow priced FAR bonuses.

### 15. Major Barriers
Legal instrument absence; RAJUK's institutional interests; the risk that priced FAR becomes a discretionary favour rather than a transparent market; and land acquisition politics if it strays beyond state-owned land.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**8/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**8/10.**

### 18. Why Now?
The metro network is being built *right now*. Development rights over station areas are worth vastly more before the lines open than after — the value is captured in the window between construction and operation, and that window is closing line by line.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** two MRT-6 station precincts on government-owned land.
- **Users:** developers; ultimately tens of thousands of residents/workers.
- **Partners:** DMTCL, RAJUK, a DFI (IFC/ADB) for transaction structuring, 2–3 developers.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** Tk 5–12 crore for structuring and transaction advisory (the capital comes from developers).
- **KPIs:** rights value realised per site; competitive bidders per site; affordable units delivered; proceeds transferred to the ring-fenced fund.
- **Success:** one competitively tendered station-area development with proceeds visibly credited to a transit fund.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
Not a startup product — but a strong **advisory and development** opportunity. Build first: station-area **market and valuation analytics** (which nobody in Bangladesh publishes), sold to developers and lenders, which positions you as the transaction adviser when rights are tendered. First customers: real estate developers and banks lending against Dhaka land. Moat: proprietary transaction and valuation data. Scale: analytics → transaction advisory → co-development equity.

## Opportunity #13 — Deposit return scheme and packaging EPR

### 1. The Core Idea
A producer-funded deposit return scheme (DRS) for beverage containers: consumers pay a small refundable deposit, return the empty to a shop or reverse-vending point, and get the deposit back; a producer-owned clearing company runs the logistics and sells the recovered material. Layered with extended producer responsibility (EPR) obligations for other packaging.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 20, 108. Dhaka alone generates ~**7,000 tonnes/day** of waste; only about **15% of inorganic waste (~475 t/day)** is recycled, and that is done by roughly **120,000 informal waste pickers** who are "neither recognised nor supported officially". [VERIFIED] Plastic leakage into rivers and drains is a visible driver of urban flooding and marine pollution.

### 3. International Benchmark
- **Norway — Infinitum** (producer-owned DRS clearing company, current form from 1999).
- **Germany — DPG deposit system** (2003).
- **Lithuania, Estonia, Netherlands, Australia (NSW Return and Earn, 2017)** — recent implementations, including in mixed-income contexts.

### 4. Proof That It Works
Norway achieved a **92.3% total return rate in 2023** across cans and plastic bottles, with an expected path to 93%; **Germany's return rates exceed 98%**. High-performing DRS systems consistently exceed **90% collection**. These are among the most reliable, repeatedly replicated outcome statistics in environmental policy. [VERIFIED]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Absent (A) for DRS; Fragmented (C) for recycling generally.** Bangladesh has a large, efficient *informal* PET recovery chain (PET bottle recovery rates in Bangladesh are actually high by regional standards because of the informal sector), some export of flakes, and a 2002 polythene bag ban that is unevenly enforced. There is no deposit system and no functioning EPR obligation. [ASSUMPTION on EPR status — verify with the Department of Environment; VERIFIED on informal sector scale.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Bangladesh's informal collection network is an **asset, not an obstacle** — it is a pre-built, low-cost reverse-logistics workforce that Norway had to create from scratch with expensive machines. A Bangladeshi DRS should pay that workforce the deposit margin rather than replace it with reverse-vending machines. Done this way, Bangladesh could reach high collection rates at a fraction of European capital cost, and simultaneously formalise and raise the income of 120,000 of the poorest urban workers.

### 7. Localization
- **People, not machines.** Reverse-vending machines cost more than the labour they replace in Bangladesh. Use manual redemption points at existing retail and at consolidation depots, with digital verification.
- **Pay the deposit via MFS.** With 239.3m MFS accounts, refunds can be instant and traceable without cash handling — this is a genuine Bangladeshi advantage over the European model.
- **Register waste pickers as the collection workforce** with ID, gloves, and a guaranteed price — converting an exploited informal labour force into the system's backbone.
- **Start with PET and aluminium only.** Glass is heavy, low-value and dangerous to handle; multi-material complexity kills early-stage schemes.
- **Producer-owned, not-for-profit clearing company** (Infinitum's structure) so producers accept the levy as their own cost-control mechanism rather than a tax.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**Industry consortium** of beverage producers (the Infinitum model) under a **government mandate**; with **social enterprises/NGOs** organising the collector workforce and **private recyclers** taking the material.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
Self-financing by design: **unredeemed deposits + material sales + a producer fee** cover operations. This is the key attraction — mature DRS systems run without ongoing subsidy.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** beverage producers (fee) and consumers (refundable deposit — not a real cost if returned).
- **Uses:** consumers, retailers, waste pickers, recyclers.
- **Benefits:** waste pickers (income and recognition), cities (lower collection and drain-clearing cost), rivers, and recyclers (clean feedstock).

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** Bangladesh's beverage container volume is not published reliably [ASSUMPTION]. Anchoring instead on the waste side: 7,000 t/day in Dhaka, ~15% inorganic recycling → the unrecycled inorganic fraction is the addressable stream.
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** if a national scheme handles 3–5 billion containers/yr at an all-in system cost of Tk 1.5–3.0 per container → a **Tk 4.5–15bn/yr (USD 37–123m/yr)** system, funded by producers and unredeemed deposits.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** a Dhaka-only launch handling 500m–1bn containers/yr → Tk 0.75–3bn/yr.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
- **Income:** 120,000 informal collectors moved onto a guaranteed price — one of the most directly pro-poor interventions available.
- **Municipal cost:** less waste to collect and less drain-blocking, which is a direct urban flooding cost.
- **Industry:** domestic recycled-PET feedstock for textile and packaging (links to Opp 50).
- **Climate:** avoided virgin plastic production.

### 13. Technology Required
Deliberately light: barcode/QR verification, MFS payout integration, depot inventory management, and fraud controls (preventing redemption of containers that never carried a deposit — the main integrity risk). No reverse-vending machines required at launch.

### 14. Government Role
- **Department of Environment**, Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change; city corporations; NBR (for the deposit's tax treatment, which must be clarified or the scheme is unworkable).
- **Government:** mandate the deposit and EPR obligation; set targets; approve the clearing company.
- **Private:** everything else.
- **PPP:** the clearing company is best structured as an industry-owned non-profit under regulation.
- **Policy change:** an EPR rule with a beverage-container deposit mandate.

### 15. Major Barriers
Producer resistance to the levy (the standard barrier everywhere); fraud in redemption; the tax treatment of deposits; and the risk of disrupting the informal chain's existing income if design is careless.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**6/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**7/10.**

### 18. Why Now?
Global brand owners face packaging commitments and plastic-treaty pressure and are now willing to fund EPR in developing markets; Bangladesh's urban flooding is politically salient; and MFS penetration makes micro-refunds operationally possible for the first time.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** two Dhaka city corporation zones plus Cox's Bazar (high tourist container volume, visible leakage).
- **Users:** 500–1,500 retail redemption points, 3,000–8,000 registered collectors.
- **Partners:** 3–5 beverage producers, DoE, city corporations, an MFS provider, a PET recycler, a waste-picker organisation.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** Tk 15–30 crore for the clearing entity, depots and systems.
- **KPIs:** return rate by material; cost per container returned; collector earnings per day; contamination rate of recovered material.
- **Success:** ≥70% return rate in the pilot zones with a system cost per container that producers accept for national rollout.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
Strong fit — and it is a business that can start **before** any mandate. Build first: an **aggregation and traceability company** that buys PET from informal collectors at a guaranteed price, bales it, verifies chain of custody, and sells certified recycled feedstock to brands who need documented recycled content. That is a real business today, driven by brand commitments, and it makes you the natural operator of a national scheme later. First customers: multinational beverage and apparel brands with recycled-content targets. Moat: the collector network and verified chain-of-custody data. Scale: PET aggregation → multi-material → clearing-company operator → export the model to South and Southeast Asia.

## Opportunity #14 — Decentralised municipal waste processing (the Indore model)

### 1. The Core Idea
Shift municipal waste from "collect and dump" to **source segregation plus decentralised processing**: households separate wet and dry waste, collection vehicles keep the streams separate, wet waste goes to ward-level composting or bio-methanation, dry waste to material recovery facilities, and only the residue goes to landfill. The revenue comes from compost, biogas/CNG, recovered materials and refuse-derived fuel to cement kilns.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 21, 30. Dhaka's two main landfills receive **~1,653 t/day (Matuail)** and **~839 t/day (Aminbazar)**; the two city corporations together handle **>7,500 tonnes/day** with most going to four landfills; only ~**15% of inorganic waste** is recovered, all informally. [VERIFIED] Landfill sites are at capacity, methane emissions are unmanaged, and leachate contaminates groundwater.

### 3. International Benchmark
- **India — Indore Municipal Corporation** (from ~2016): ranked India's cleanest city for years running under Swachh Survekshan, achieved near-universal source segregation and operates a large bio-CNG plant on segregated wet waste.
- **Indonesia — Surabaya's community composting (Takakura method)**.
- **South Korea — volume-based waste fee (jongnyangje, 1995)**, the most effective demand-side instrument globally.
- **Brazil — waste-picker cooperatives integrated into municipal collection** (Belo Horizonte).

### 4. Proof That It Works
Indore is the most-documented South Asian case of a city moving to near-total source segregation and decentralised processing within a few years, sustained across multiple administrations, and is the reference model in India's national urban sanitation programme. [VERIFIED that Indore achieved and sustained top ranking in India's national cleanliness survey and operates bio-CNG on segregated waste; specific tonnage and revenue figures should be taken from Indore Municipal Corporation or Swachh Survekshan reports before quoting in a memo.] Korea's volume-based fee is the best-evidenced instrument for reducing waste generation at source.

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Underperforming (D).** Bangladesh has city corporation collection (largely via primary collection service providers), some NGO-run composting (Waste Concern's work is internationally cited and is a genuine Bangladeshi innovation), and a landfill-centric system. Source segregation is essentially absent at scale, and city corporations pay contractors by tonnage hauled — which financially *rewards* not diverting waste. [VERIFIED on landfill tonnages; ASSUMPTION on contract structure — verify with DNCC/DSCC.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
The single highest-leverage change is not technology — it is **changing what the city pays for**. Bangladesh already has Waste Concern's decentralised composting model as a domestic proof point that was internationally recognised and then never scaled. Meanwhile organic content in Bangladeshi municipal waste is very high (typical of South Asian cities), which makes wet-waste processing economically better here than in Europe. And landfill exhaustion in Dhaka makes the alternative unavoidable rather than optional.

### 7. Localization
- **Change the contract, then the technology.** Pay collection contractors per tonne *diverted from landfill*, not per tonne hauled. Nothing else matters until this changes.
- **Two-bin, not five-bin.** Wet/dry only. Multi-stream segregation fails in dense low-income settlements everywhere it has been tried.
- **Integrate, do not displace, the informal sector.** Waste pickers already do the dry-waste recovery; formalise them into the material recovery facilities (the Brazilian cooperative model) rather than competing with them.
- **Ward-level composting, not mega-plants.** Bangladesh's traffic makes centralised haulage expensive; small plants near the source also survive political change better than mega-projects.
- **Compost offtake must be contracted upfront** — the graveyard of South Asian composting is unsold compost. Tie it to the fertiliser distribution system or to tea/horticulture estates before building.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**PPP** between city corporations and private operators, with **NGO/social enterprise** partners for behaviour change and waste-picker organisation. Cement companies as RDF offtakers. **Waste Concern** is the obvious domestic anchor partner.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Municipal tipping/service fee + material and compost sales + carbon credits + RDF offtake.** Household user fees already exist informally in Bangladesh (residents pay primary collection providers) and can be formalised. Carbon finance for avoided methane is genuinely material for this asset class.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** city corporations, households (collection fee), cement kilns (RDF), farmers (compost).
- **Uses:** households, collectors, processors.
- **Benefits:** the city (landfill life extended), residents (air, drains, flooding), waste pickers (formal jobs).

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** Dhaka's >7,500 t/day is **~2.7m tonnes/yr**. At an all-in processing value of Tk 1,500–3,000/tonne → **Tk 4–8bn/yr (USD 33–66m/yr)** for Dhaka alone.
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** national urban waste is several times Dhaka's; a national market of **Tk 15–30bn/yr** is plausible as segregation spreads to other city corporations.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** one operator running 200–500 t/day → Tk 300m–800m/yr.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
Landfill life, methane (a high-GWP emission with real carbon value), urban flooding via unblocked drains, and formal employment for informal waste workers. Air pollution reduction has an outsized value given the **8.3% of GDP** health cost attributed to air pollution. [VERIFIED]

### 13. Technology Required
Deliberately simple: segregated collection vehicles, windrow or in-vessel composting, small bio-methanation, material recovery sorting lines, weighbridges and route tracking for verification. High technology is a trap in this sector — several South Asian waste-to-energy plants have failed on high-moisture, low-calorific-value feedstock.

### 14. Government Role
- **Local Government Division**, city corporations and pourashavas; Department of Environment.
- **Government:** mandate source segregation; restructure collection contracts to pay for diversion; enforce landfill discipline.
- **Private:** collect, process, sell outputs.
- **PPP:** yes, with performance-based payment.
- **Policy change:** a solid waste management rule mandating segregation at source and diversion targets per city corporation.

### 15. Major Barriers
Household behaviour change (slow, and requires sustained enforcement); the contractor lobby whose revenue depends on tonnage; compost offtake risk; and municipal payment reliability, which is the single biggest killer of urban PPPs in South Asia.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**7/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**7/10.**

### 18. Why Now?
Dhaka's landfills are approaching capacity; carbon finance for methane avoidance is now accessible; and Indore has provided a nearby, culturally proximate, replicable template that did not exist a decade ago.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** 2–3 wards of one city corporation (~150,000–300,000 residents) — ward-level is the right unit, because it is small enough to enforce and large enough to prove economics.
- **Users:** all households in the pilot wards.
- **Partners:** city corporation, Waste Concern or equivalent, a cement company for RDF, a compost distributor, a carbon project developer.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** Tk 12–25 crore.
- **KPIs:** source segregation compliance %; tonnes diverted from landfill; compost sold; cost per tonne processed vs the city's current landfill cost; collector incomes.
- **Success:** >60% segregation compliance and a processing cost at or below the city's current haul-and-dump cost — the second condition is what makes it scalable.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
Realistic and capital-light at ward scale. Build first: a **ward-level wet-waste processing operation with contracted compost offtake**, plus a dry-waste material recovery facility staffed by organised waste pickers. First customer: one city corporation ward contract, plus a private compost buyer. Moat: municipal contracts are sticky, and operational know-how in high-moisture composting is genuinely scarce. Scale: ward → city → multi-city → carbon-financed national platform.

## Opportunity #15 — Faecal sludge management as a scheduled utility service

### 1. The Core Idea
Turn septic-tank emptying from an ad-hoc informal service into a **scheduled desludging utility**: every non-sewered property is on a rotation, pays a small monthly tariff on its water bill, and is emptied on a published schedule by licensed operators who must discharge at a treatment plant with a manifest. Treated output is sold as soil conditioner or fuel.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gap 22, and it interacts with gap 32. Most of urban Bangladesh outside central Dhaka has no sewerage; septic tanks and pits are emptied informally and the sludge is discharged into drains, rivers and canals. This is a primary route for faecal contamination of surface and shallow groundwater in exactly the settlements that also depend on shallow tubewells. [ASSUMPTION on the specific national coverage numbers — the Department of Public Health Engineering and city corporations hold this data and it must be verified before investment; the structural situation is well documented in Bangladesh's own Institutional and Regulatory Framework for FSM.]

### 3. International Benchmark
- **Senegal — Dakar's scheduled desludging and the Omni Processor** (ONAS with the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, from ~2011): call-centre-based desludging market plus treatment.
- **India — Wai and Sinnar, Maharashtra; Devanahalli, Karnataka** — the first Indian towns to run city-wide scheduled desludging with treatment, now the basis of India's national FSSM policy (2017).
- **Malaysia — Indah Water Konsortium** (1994): the most mature national scheduled-desludging utility in the world.

Malaysia's IWK has run a national scheduled desludging service for three decades — the strongest existing proof that this can be a *utility*, not a project. Dakar's programme demonstrated that formalising the desludging market lowers prices to households while improving disposal compliance. [VERIFIED that these programmes exist at the stated scale and vintage; specific coverage percentages should be sourced from the respective utilities and India's MoHUA before quoting.]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Early-stage (B).** Bangladesh has an approved Institutional and Regulatory Framework for FSM (2017) and a number of donor-supported FSM projects and treatment plants (including in Faridpur, Jhenaidah, Sakhipur and Kushtia — Bangladesh actually has some of South Asia's better-documented small-town FSM pilots). What is missing is **scheduled service with tariff-based financing at scale**, rather than demand-responsive emptying supported by project funds. [VERIFIED that Bangladesh has FSM pilots and a policy framework; ASSUMPTION on current coverage.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Bangladesh will not sewer its secondary cities this century — the capital cost is prohibitive. FSM is not a stopgap; it is the *permanent* sanitation model for most of the country, and it costs roughly an order of magnitude less than sewerage per household served. Bangladesh also already has the pilots and the policy; what it lacks is the utility business model. That gap is exactly what a private operator can fill.

### 7. Localization
- **Bill on the water or holding tax bill.** A separate sanitation bill will not be paid. This is the single most important design decision and it requires a municipal ordinance, not new technology.
- **Scheduled, not on-call.** On-call emptying means households defer until failure and then use the cheapest illegal option. Scheduled rotation is what makes the economics and the public health outcome work.
- **License and equip the existing informal emptiers** rather than displacing them — they have the access knowledge for narrow lanes that vacuum trucks cannot enter. Small vacutugs, not large tankers.
- **Co-treat with organic municipal waste** (Opp 14) to share capital cost and improve compost quality.
- **Design for high water tables and flooding** — Bangladesh-specific: many pilots elsewhere assume drying beds that do not work in monsoon conditions; covered or mechanical dewatering is required.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**PPP** between pourashavas/city corporations and private operators; **social enterprises** for the emptier organisation; **DPHE** for technical standards; DFIs for treatment plant capital.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Tariff-based utility revenue** (monthly sanitation charge) + **treatment gate fees** + sale of treated biosolids/compost and, where volumes allow, fuel. Capital for treatment plants from municipal or DFI sources; operations self-financing.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** households and businesses via a small monthly tariff.
- **Uses:** households, emptiers, the treatment operator.
- **Benefits:** everyone downstream — this is a classic public health externality — plus emptiers (formal, safer work) and farmers (soil conditioner).

- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** one operator covering 100,000–300,000 households → Tk 50–360m/yr.

The World Bank's finding that **unsafe water, sanitation and hygiene plus air pollution and lead cost ~17.6% of GDP** frames the scale of the prize. [VERIFIED]

## Opportunity #16 — An energy services company (ESCO) market with measurement and verification

### 1. The Core Idea
Create a market for **energy savings performance contracting**: an ESCO installs efficiency upgrades in a factory, hospital or commercial building at its own cost and is repaid from a share of the verified energy savings. The enabling infrastructure is a credible measurement-and-verification (M&V) standard and a contract form that banks will lend against.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 23, 24. Bangladesh is carrying ~**40% power overcapacity** (peak 16,603 MW against 27,414 MW installed) while **BPDB losses reached Tk 50,565 crore in FY25** and capacity payments have been a massive fiscal drain — over Tk 1.04 lakh crore paid to IPPs and rental plants over 14 years. [VERIFIED] The cheapest megawatt in this system is the one not consumed, and industrial energy intensity is high.

### 3. International Benchmark
- **India — Energy Efficiency Services Limited (EESL)** (2009), a public ESCO that scaled LED and agricultural pump programmes through bulk procurement and pay-from-savings.
- **China — ESCO/EMC industry** developed under the World Bank/GEF China Energy Conservation programme from the late 1990s into one of the world's largest.
- **USA — the federal ESPC programme** and the **IPMVP** measurement protocol (the global M&V standard).

### 4. Proof That It Works
EESL's UJALA LED programme is one of the largest efficiency deployments ever undertaken, and drove down LED prices in India by an order of magnitude through demand aggregation — the mechanism is well documented in IEA and World Bank material. China's ESCO industry grew from a World Bank-supported pilot into a multi-billion-dollar sector, and is the standard citation for "ESCO markets can be created by policy in a developing economy". [VERIFIED that both programmes exist at the stated scale and vintage; specific savings figures should be sourced from EESL annual reports and IEA before quoting.]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Absent (A) as a market.** Bangladesh has SREDA with an energy efficiency and conservation mandate, some IFC/IDCOL-supported efficiency lending, and individual factory efficiency projects (particularly in RMG, driven by brand requirements and LEED certification — Bangladesh has a globally notable number of LEED-certified garment factories). But there is no ESCO industry, no standard M&V protocol in local practice, and no bankable savings-based contract form. [VERIFIED on overcapacity and losses; ASSUMPTION on ESCO market absence — verify with SREDA.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Bangladesh has an unusual combination: a large, concentrated, export-oriented industrial base that is **already under buyer pressure to decarbonise**, plus a power sector whose fiscal position makes demand reduction attractive to the state, plus high energy prices post-subsidy-reform. The RMG sector's existing sustainability reporting obligations solve the ESCO industry's usual first problem — persuading customers to care.

### 7. Localization
- **Anchor in RMG and textiles**, where buyer decarbonisation requirements create willingness to pay that would not otherwise exist. Boilers, steam systems, compressed air and motors — not exotic technology.
- **Standardise the contract and the M&V protocol nationally** (adopt IPMVP) so banks can underwrite savings streams. Without this, every deal is bespoke and the market never scales.
- **Aggregate small projects into portfolios** for financing — individual factory projects are too small to interest banks.
- **Use a partial credit guarantee** to cover savings-performance risk in the first cohort; this is the standard market-creation instrument and Bangladesh Bank has run guarantee schemes before (the SME Credit Guarantee Scheme).
- **Link to rooftop solar (Opp 17)** — efficiency plus generation sold as one contract has better economics and one sales conversation.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**Startups and SMEs** as ESCOs; **large corporations** (engineering firms) as ESCOs for big projects; **banks and IDCOL** as financiers; **SREDA** as standard-setter; **BGMEA/BKMEA** as the channel.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Shared savings or guaranteed savings contracts** — the ESCO takes a share of verified savings for 3–7 years. Financing via bank debt against the savings contract, ideally with a guarantee facility. Also: **B2B project delivery** and M&V-as-a-service.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** industrial and commercial energy consumers, out of savings (so net cost is negative).
- **Uses:** factories, hospitals, hotels, commercial buildings.
- **Benefits:** factory owners (lower cost, buyer compliance), the state (reduced generation and capacity burden), the grid, and air quality.

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** if Bangladesh's industrial and commercial sector could economically save 15–20% of its electricity and gas consumption, and the installed base is being served by a system with 27,414 MW capacity, the avoided-energy value runs to **tens of billions of taka per year** [CALCULATED at a coarse level; a bottom-up energy balance is required before investment].
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** 7,000+ RMG factories [VERIFIED]. If 1,500 undertake projects averaging Tk 1.5 crore → a **Tk 2,250 crore (USD 184m)** project market, generating recurring shared-savings revenue.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** an ESCO doing 30–60 projects/yr → Tk 45–90 crore/yr of project revenue.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
Fiscal (reduced pressure to generate), industrial competitiveness (energy is a major cost line post-LDC when tariff preferences vanish), export compliance (buyer decarbonisation requirements are becoming a market-access condition), and emissions.

Sub-metering and IoT energy monitoring, variable frequency drives, boiler and steam system upgrades, compressed-air optimisation, LED and HVAC, plus an M&V data platform. Off-the-shelf; the innovation is contractual and financial.

### 14. Government Role
- **SREDA**, Power Division, Bangladesh Bank (guarantee and refinance), BIDA.
- **Government:** adopt an M&V standard; create a standard ESPC contract; offer a partial credit guarantee; mandate energy audits above a consumption threshold (SREDA already has powers here).
- **Private:** everything else.
- **PPP:** not required, but a public guarantee facility is.
- **Policy change:** energy audit mandate enforcement and a savings-contract legal form.

### 15. Major Barriers
Customer trust in savings claims (universal — solved only by credible M&V); bank unfamiliarity with cash-flow lending against savings; measurement disputes; and the fact that when electricity supply is unreliable, factories prioritise availability over efficiency.

**5/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**7/10.**

Energy prices have risen with subsidy rationalisation; the fiscal cost of overcapacity is now a public issue; and RMG buyers' decarbonisation deadlines fall inside 2026–2030. All three point the same way at the same time.

## Opportunity #17 — Industrial rooftop and floating solar under a RESCO/OPEX model

### 1. The Core Idea
Install solar on factory rooftops and on water bodies at **zero capital cost to the host**, under a 15–25 year power purchase agreement where the developer owns the asset and sells power at a tariff below the grid rate. The host saves money from day one; the developer earns a long-dated, contracted, hard-asset-backed cash flow.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 24, 25. Bangladesh has **7,000+ RMG factories with large suitable rooftops**, and IDCOL has estimated that fully utilising RMG, textile and industrial rooftops could yield around **5,000 MW** of solar capacity. Yet only about **116 MW** is installed under net metering across **2,657 systems**, plus a further ~94 MW from 264 systems outside net metering. [VERIFIED] That is roughly **2% of the identified potential**.

### 3. International Benchmark
- **India — rooftop solar RESCO/OPEX market** and **PM Surya Ghar** (2024) for residential; **SECI and state open-access C&I solar** for industry.
- **India — PM-KUSUM** (2019) for agricultural solarisation, and **Gujarat/Kerala canal-top and floating solar**.
- **Vietnam** — a rooftop solar boom (2019–2020) driven by feed-in tariffs, which is also a cautionary tale about grid integration.

### 4. Proof That It Works
India's off-grid/PM-KUSUM segment reached **5,014 MW of installed capacity by 31 May 2025**, with **over 828,260 pumps installed under Component B** (~62% of the 1.3m sanctioned) and, in FY25 alone, **Component B installations up 4.2× to 440,000 pumps and Component C solarisations up 25× to 260,000** — the fastest single-year growth since launch, with more than one million pumps installed or solarised by 2025. [VERIFIED] This demonstrates that a policy-created solar market in a South Asian regulatory context can scale extremely fast once financing and offtake are resolved.

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Early-stage (B), with a policy inflection just completed.** The **Net Metering Guidelines 2025** raised sanctioned-load eligibility from 70% to 100% and opened the scheme to single-phase consumers; a **December 2025 Power Division circular made rooftop solar mandatory for new electricity connections for buildings with at least 1,000 sq ft of usable roof**; and the Renewable Energy Policy 2025 is in place. IDCOL has a long track record in solar (the Solar Home Systems programme was one of the largest in the world). So the enabling framework has just changed materially — the market has not yet responded. [VERIFIED]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
This is arguably the best **timing** case in the dossier: a large, identified, unbuilt resource (~5,000 MW), a customer base that is concentrated and creditworthy (export factories), a buyer-driven decarbonisation mandate, expensive grid power, and a regulatory regime that was liberalised within the last 12 months. The binding constraint has been capex on the factory balance sheet — which the RESCO model removes entirely.

### 7. Localization
- **OPEX/RESCO, not CAPEX.** Bangladeshi factory owners will not put solar on their balance sheet when working capital is scarce; they will sign a PPA that saves money immediately. This single structural choice is the difference between 2% and 40% penetration.
- **Underwrite the offtaker, not the roof.** Factory credit risk is the real risk. Structure around export-oriented factories with visible buyer relationships, and use escrow of a portion of export proceeds where possible.
- **Roof structural assessment is a real constraint** — many older Bangladeshi factory sheds cannot carry conventional mounting. Lightweight mounting and structural strengthening must be part of the product, and it is where local engineering value-add sits.
- **Floating solar on water bodies** (ponds, reservoirs, and the many water bodies attached to industrial estates) is a genuine Bangladeshi fit given land scarcity — India's floating solar experience is directly transferable.
- **Bundle with efficiency (Opp 16)** and sell "energy cost reduction", not "solar".

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**Startups/SMEs** as RESCOs; **large corporations** and **IPPs** for utility-scale and floating; **IDCOL and banks** as financiers; **PPP** for public-building rooftops and canal/waterbody sites.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**PPA / energy-as-a-service** — long-term contracted tariff, project-financed. Secondary revenue: renewable energy certificates or buyer-funded decarbonisation premiums, and eventually carbon credits.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** factories and commercial hosts, at below grid tariff.
- **Uses:** industrial energy consumers.
- **Benefits:** factories (cost + compliance), the grid (reduced peak), the Treasury (less fuel import and less capacity payment pressure), and export competitiveness.

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** 5,000 MW of identified industrial rooftop potential [VERIFIED estimate by IDCOL]. At an installed cost of roughly USD 550–750/kW → **USD 2.75–3.75bn (Tk 335–458bn)** of capital deployment.
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** a realistic 1,000–1,500 MW addressable in the next decade → **USD 550m–1.1bn** of capex, generating roughly **USD 90–170m/yr** of PPA revenue [CALCULATED at ~1,400 kWh/kWp/yr and ~USD 0.09/kWh].
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** a leading RESCO reaching 100–200 MW → USD 12–25m/yr of contracted revenue.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
Foreign exchange (displaced LNG and fuel imports — directly relevant given Bangladesh's reserve pressures), industrial cost competitiveness post-LDC, emissions, and grid peak relief. Employment in installation and O&M is meaningful and regionally distributed.

### 13. Technology Required
PV modules and inverters, lightweight mounting systems, remote monitoring, net metering interface, and — for floating — anchoring and mooring engineering. Battery storage becomes relevant later for peak shifting.

### 14. Government Role
- **SREDA**, Power Division, BPDB/DESCO/DPDC/BREB (net metering execution), BERC (tariff), Bangladesh Bank (refinance lines).
- **Government:** make net metering approval fast and predictable — approval latency, not policy, is the practical barrier; enforce the new mandatory-rooftop circular; allow third-party sale/wheeling, which is the single most valuable remaining reform.
- **Private:** finance, build, operate.
- **PPP:** for public rooftops and water bodies.
- **Policy change:** open access / third-party PPA rights for behind-the-meter and wheeled solar.

### 15. Major Barriers
Offtaker credit risk; net metering approval delays at the distribution utility; the absence of third-party sale rights in some configurations; roof structural condition; and long-tenor local-currency financing availability.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**4/10** — the lowest-difficulty large opportunity in this dossier.

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**9/10.**

### 18. Why Now?
The Net Metering Guidelines 2025 and the December 2025 mandatory-rooftop circular changed the rules inside the last year; grid tariffs have risen; and RMG buyers' 2030 emissions commitments make factory renewables a market-access issue rather than a nice-to-have. The first movers will lock in the best roofs and the best offtakers.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** Gazipur, Savar, Narayanganj, Chattogram EPZ area.
- **Users:** 5–15 factories, 5–15 MW total.
- **Partners:** an RMG group, IDCOL or a bank, an EPC contractor, a module supplier, and a brand willing to co-sign decarbonisation value.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** USD 3–9m of project capital.
- **KPIs:** MW commissioned; PPA tariff vs grid tariff; net metering approval time; plant load factor achieved; collection rate from hosts.
- **Success:** 5 MW+ operating with collections at 100% and a repeatable financing structure that a bank will replicate without a new credit committee process.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
The single most executable large opportunity here for a Bangladeshi company. First customer: one large RMG group with multiple factories — one relationship, many roofs. Moat: long-tenor PPAs are themselves the moat (25-year contracted cash flows), plus access to cheap long-term capital, which is the real competitive variable. ## Opportunity #18 — Inland waterway container feeder and river port modernisation

### 1. The Core Idea
Move containers between Chattogram/Matarbari and inland destinations by river on scheduled, standardised barge services with modern inland container terminals — turning Bangladesh's ~3,800–6,000 km of navigable waterway from a legacy bulk-cargo system into a container logistics corridor.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 26, 27, 103. **Roads carry 96% of Bangladesh's freight; rail 3%; inland waterways 1%** — against a government target of 20% for rail. Chattogram port's import container dwell has been reported at **20–25 days versus a 4-day international norm**, and the port ranked **341st of 370** in the 2021 Container Port Performance Index. [VERIFIED — the CPPI figure is from 2021 and should be refreshed against the latest CPPI before publication.] Bangladesh is one of the most riverine countries on earth and uses its rivers for almost no containerised freight.

### 3. International Benchmark
- **Netherlands/Germany — Rhine barge container network**: roughly a third of Rotterdam's hinterland container volume moves by barge.
- **Vietnam — Mekong Delta and Cai Mep feeder barges** connecting Ho Chi Minh City and the delta to deep-sea terminals.
- **Bangladesh–India Protocol on Inland Water Transit and Trade** — an existing bilateral instrument that already permits river cargo movement between the two countries. [VERIFIED that the protocol exists]

### 4. Proof That It Works
The Rhine corridor is the global reference for inland waterway containerisation and is the reason Rotterdam can serve a continental hinterland without gridlocking its road network; barge modal shares of ~30%+ on that corridor are standard in European port statistics. Vietnam's delta barge feeders demonstrate the model in a monsoon-climate, developing-economy setting with similar draft constraints to Bangladesh. [VERIFIED that both systems operate at scale; specific modal-share figures should be sourced from Port of Rotterdam and Vietnamese Maritime Administration statistics before quoting.]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Underperforming (D).** Bangladesh has BIWTA, a large inland vessel fleet, existing river ports, Pangaon Inland Container Terminal near Dhaka (built specifically for this purpose and chronically underutilised), and the India protocol routes. The infrastructure partly exists and is not used — which is a very different problem from absence, and points to service design, customs process and reliability rather than capital.

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Pangaon's underutilisation is the most instructive fact in this entire opportunity: Bangladesh built the terminal and did not build the *service*. What is missing is scheduled, reliable, competitively priced barge service with integrated customs clearance — a logistics business, not a construction project. Meanwhile road congestion on the Dhaka–Chattogram corridor and the arrival of Matarbari deep-sea port make the economics increasingly favourable.

### 7. Localization
- **Sell reliability, not price.** Barge is slower; it wins only if it is *predictable*. Fixed-day scheduled sailings, published transit times, and a service guarantee.
- **Solve customs at the inland terminal**, not at the port — the single largest reason Pangaon underperforms is process, not water depth.
- **Design for draft and siltation**: shallow-draft, wide-beam barges purpose-designed for Bangladeshi rivers, plus a maintenance dredging contract with performance obligations, not ad-hoc dredging.
- **Anchor with a captive shipper** — an RMG group, a cement or steel importer, or an FMCG company with regular Dhaka-bound volume — before ordering vessels.
- **Target the India protocol routes** for a second revenue leg (Kolkata/Haldia–Bangladesh and transit to the North-East Indian states), which is a genuinely differentiated market.

**Private logistics companies and shipping lines**, with **PPP** for terminal concessions, and **BIWTA** for channel maintenance. Existing Bangladeshi shipping groups are the natural incumbents.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Freight revenue per TEU**, terminal handling charges, and warehousing/CFS services at the inland terminal. Asset-heavy but with contracted volume it is a classic infrastructure yield business.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** shippers and freight forwarders.
- **Uses:** importers/exporters, especially RMG.
- **Benefits:** shippers (cost and predictability), the road network (reduced heavy truck damage), air quality, and Chattogram port (relieved of dwell pressure).

Capital-intensive, so entry should be **asset-light first**. Build first: a **freight brokerage and multimodal booking platform** that sells river-barge capacity chartered from existing operators, with digital tracking and guaranteed schedules — proving demand before buying vessels. First customers: RMG exporters and importers of bulk consumer goods. Moat: shipper contracts, terminal access, and schedule reliability data. Scale: brokerage → chartered scheduled service → owned vessels and terminal concession.

## Opportunity #19 — Planned migrant-receiving secondary cities

### 1. The Core Idea
Instead of allowing climate-displaced people to arrive unplanned in Dhaka slums, deliberately develop a small number of **secondary cities as receiving destinations**: pre-provisioned serviced land, rental housing, an industrial anchor employer, vocational training, and transport connectivity — turning displacement into planned urbanisation.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gaps 28, 31, 106. Projections put **up to 13.3 million Bangladeshis displaced by climate impacts by 2050**, with **more than 35 million coastal residents at risk**, and salinity alone potentially displacing **up to 200,000** people. [VERIFIED — these are projections, and should be presented as such.] The default destination is Dhaka's slums, which imposes enormous costs on both the migrants and the city.

### 3. International Benchmark
- **Türkiye — TOKİ (Housing Development Administration)**: large-scale state-led social housing production.
- **China — new town and industrial-park-led urbanisation** (the mechanism, not the political model).
- **Singapore — HDB** (1960): the reference case for state-produced affordable housing at national scale.
- **Bangladesh — Mongla** is the domestically discussed candidate: a port city with an export processing zone that has been explicitly promoted as a climate-migration destination. [VERIFIED that Mongla is discussed in this role in the climate-migration literature]
- **Ethiopia/Vietnam — industrial park-anchored secondary city growth**.

### 4. Proof That It Works
Singapore's HDB is the strongest proof that state-led housing production at scale can work, though its fiscal and land-ownership preconditions do not transfer. TOKİ demonstrates large-volume delivery in a middle-income democracy. Vietnam's industrial-park-led secondary city growth (Bac Ninh, Binh Duong) is the most transferable: **jobs first, then housing, then services** — the sequencing lesson. [VERIFIED that these programmes exist at scale; this opportunity has the weakest *quantified* international evidence base in the dossier, and that should be stated plainly to any investment committee.]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Absent (A) as a coherent programme.** Bangladesh has economic zones (BEZA), the Ashrayan housing programme for the landless, and active discussion of Mongla as a climate-migrant destination. What does not exist is an integrated **jobs + serviced land + rental housing + skills** package aimed at receiving migrants in specific towns. [ASSUMPTION — verify with BEZA and the Ministry of Housing and Public Works.]

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Bangladesh is going to urbanise regardless; the only question is whether it happens into Dhaka's flood plains or into planned secondary cities. Doing it deliberately is far cheaper than retrofitting Dhaka. And Bangladesh has an unusual asset: **BEZA's economic zone pipeline** provides the "jobs first" anchor that most planned-city projects lack, and which is the reason most of them fail.

### 7. Localization
- **Jobs first, always.** Housing without employment produces empty estates — the failure mode of state housing programmes from Egypt to India. Anchor each city on a committed industrial employer before building homes.
- **Rental, not ownership.** Migrants need affordable rental at arrival; ownership-based programmes exclude exactly the population being targeted. This is the central departure from the TOKİ and Ashrayan models.
- **Serviced land plus incremental self-build** is far cheaper per household than finished units and matches how Bangladeshi households actually build. "Sites and services", not apartment blocks.
- **Climate-proof the destination**, or the programme relocates people from one flood risk to another — elevation, drainage and water supply are non-negotiable design inputs.
- **Target intermediate towns near origin**, not only distant ones: most displacement is short-distance, and people move where they have kin networks.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**Government-led** (BEZA, Ministry of Housing and Public Works, Local Government Division) with **PPP** for housing and utilities, **large corporations** as anchor employers, and **NGOs** for settlement support.

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Land value + rental income + industrial lease revenue.** Serviced-plot sales and long-lease industrial land cross-subsidise affordable rental. Rental housing can be financed as an infrastructure asset with an institutional investor if occupancy is anchored by employer demand.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** industrial tenants (land lease), residents (rent), government (trunk infrastructure).
- **Uses:** migrants, employers.
- **Benefits:** migrants (jobs and secure housing), Dhaka (reduced in-migration pressure), employers (labour supply).

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** if even 2 million households relocate over 2026–2040 and each requires Tk 6–12 lakh of serviced land and basic housing, the capital requirement is **Tk 1.2–2.4 trillion (USD 10–20bn)** — a national-scale programme, not a project.
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** one city programme housing 100,000 households → **Tk 60–120bn (USD 490m–1bn)** of development.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** a first phase of 5,000–15,000 households → Tk 3–18bn.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
Urbanisation quality, Dhaka congestion relief, climate resilience, and industrial decentralisation (which also addresses the concentration risk of having most export capacity in one metropolitan belt). Employment effects are large and direct.

### 13. Technology Required
Modest and deliberately so: precast and modular construction for cost and speed, GIS-based site planning, flood modelling for site selection, and decentralised water and FSM (Opp 15). This is not a smart-city opportunity and should not be sold as one.

### 14. Government Role
- **BEZA**, Ministry of Housing and Public Works, Local Government Division, Ministry of Disaster Management and Relief, Water Development Board.
- **Government:** site selection, trunk infrastructure, land assembly, and the anchor-employer incentive package.
- **Private:** housing construction and management, utilities, industry.
- **PPP:** essential.
- **Policy change:** a rental housing legal framework — Bangladesh's tenancy law does not currently support institutional rental at scale, and this is the pivotal reform.

### 15. Major Barriers
Land acquisition; anchor-employer commitment; the very long payback; political continuity across administrations; and the risk that "planned relocation" becomes coercive if badly governed.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**9/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**8/10.**

### 18. Why Now?
Climate displacement is already occurring; BEZA's zone pipeline provides anchors now; and Dhaka's absorptive capacity is visibly exhausted. Decisions made in 2026–2030 determine where 10 million people live in 2040.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** Mongla (existing EPZ and port, actively discussed as a receiving city) and one northern town facing river erosion displacement.
- **Users:** 2,000–5,000 households.
- **Partners:** BEZA, the municipality, 1–2 anchor employers, a housing developer, an NGO for settlement support, a DFI.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** Tk 150–400 crore for a first phase.
- **KPIs:** jobs created and filled by migrant households; occupancy rate; rent as % of household income; retention at 12 months; services coverage.
- **Success:** ≥80% occupancy with ≥70% of working-age residents employed locally, and rent below 25% of household income.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
Not a startup — but there is a real company here: an **institutional rental housing operator for industrial workers**. Build first: purpose-built worker housing adjacent to an existing economic zone, leased to employers on a per-bed basis (employers already pay housing allowances and struggle with worker retention). First customer: a large factory group in an economic zone. Moat: land positions near anchor employers and operating capability in low-cost rental management, which nobody in Bangladesh has institutionalised. Scale: worker housing → mixed migrant rental → integrated township development.

## Opportunity #20 — Common effluent treatment as a performance-contracted utility

### 1. The Core Idea
Run industrial common effluent treatment plants (CETPs) as **regulated utilities under performance contracts with private operators**, paid per cubic metre treated to a defined standard, with continuous online monitoring published publicly — instead of as public assets operated on a maintenance budget. The immediate target is the Savar tannery estate.

### 2. Bangladesh Problem
Gap 29, 96. The Savar tannery estate's CETP is "poorly maintained and continues to release untreated water", and this is the direct cause of the leather sector's certification failure: **of the 1,710 points required for LWG certification across 17 criteria, 300 relate to the CETP** — and tanneries cannot score them. Only **~8 Bangladeshi institutions hold LWG certification**; around **140 of 162 allocated Savar tanneries operate** but most cannot export directly to the EU and US, so they **sell raw leather to China at lower prices** for processing and re-export. The sector is **stuck near USD 1bn against an estimated USD 5bn potential**. [VERIFIED]

### 3. International Benchmark
- **India — Tamil Nadu CETPs for tanneries** (Ranipet, Vellore) with zero-liquid-discharge mandates and Common Effluent Treatment Plant societies; and the **Kanpur/Unnao** experience as the cautionary case.
- **Italy — Santa Croce sull'Arno / Consorzio Aquarno**: the global reference for a tannery district running a shared, industry-owned, professionally operated effluent utility, in operation for decades, which is precisely the model Savar was supposed to be.
- **Türkiye — Organised Industrial Zone (OIZ) shared infrastructure model**, where zone management companies operate utilities for member firms.

### 4. Proof That It Works
Santa Croce sull'Arno is the demonstration that a dense cluster of small tanneries can achieve full European environmental compliance through a shared, industry-owned treatment consortium while remaining commercially competitive — the exact structural situation as Savar, with the opposite outcome. Türkiye's OIZ model demonstrates that industry-managed shared utilities outperform state-operated ones in a middle-income context. [VERIFIED that both models exist and are long-running; specific performance data should be sourced from Consorzio Aquarno and Turkish OIZ authorities before quoting.]

### 5. Bangladesh Current Situation
**Underperforming (D).** The CETP exists; it does not work. The government plans a new world-class CETP and possible renovation of the existing one, with large tanneries required to build their own ETPs and small ones connecting to the central system. [VERIFIED] But the announced fix is **capital**, and the diagnosed failure is **operations and accountability** — which is why this opportunity is framed as a contracting reform rather than a construction project.

### 6. The Bangladesh Opportunity
Leather is the clearest **stranded export opportunity** in Bangladesh: the raw material advantage exists (a large domestic hide supply, seasonally concentrated at Eid-ul-Azha), the manufacturing base exists, the labour cost advantage exists, and the *only* binding constraint is a certification gate that is 300 points of effluent compliance wide. The gap between USD 1bn and USD 5bn is, functionally, the value of a working treatment plant. Very few opportunities anywhere have such a narrow, identified, physical bottleneck standing in front of a USD 4bn prize.

### 7. Localization
- **Contract for outcomes, not construction.** Pay the operator per m³ treated to standard, with penalties for exceedance and public online discharge monitoring. This is the entire reform.
- **Charge tanneries a tariff**, so the plant has an operating income independent of the development budget. A free CETP will always be an unmaintained CETP.
- **Segregate chrome-bearing streams at source** — mixing them is the standard technical failure in tannery CETPs and it makes both treatment and chrome recovery impossible.
- **Recover and sell chrome and solid by-products** (a real revenue line in Italian and Indian tannery clusters) to improve the operating economics.
- **Tie certification services in**: the operator should also provide the traceability and compliance documentation that LWG audits require, since that is what tanneries actually want to buy.
- **Solve the seasonal Eid load spike**, which is Bangladesh-specific: hide volumes are extraordinarily concentrated in a few days, and a plant sized for average flow will fail annually.

### 8. Who Could Implement It?
**PPP** — Ministry of Industries / BSCIC as contracting authority, a specialised international water/effluent operator (Veolia, SUEZ, or an Indian equivalent such as VA Tech Wabag) with a local partner, and the **tannery association** as an industry consortium co-owner (the Italian structure).

### 9. Business / Funding Model
**Tariff per cubic metre treated**, plus **by-product recovery revenue** (chrome, sludge-derived products), plus **compliance and traceability services** sold to tanneries. Availability-based payment from government during ramp-up.

### 10. Target Users / Beneficiaries
- **Pays:** tanneries (tariff), initially co-funded by government.
- **Uses:** tanneries; LWG auditors; brand compliance teams.
- **Benefits:** exporters (market access), workers (occupational exposure), the Dhaleshwari river and downstream communities, and the balance of payments.

### 11. Estimated Market Potential
- **TAM [CALCULATED]:** the export gap itself — **USD 1bn actual versus USD 5bn potential** → a **USD 4bn (Tk 488bn)** unlock, of which the treatment utility captures only a small fee but enables all of it. [VERIFIED on the USD 1bn/USD 5bn figures as expert estimates]
- **SAM [CALCULATED]:** CETP tariff revenue. At an estimated 25,000 m³/day and a tariff of Tk 25–60/m³ → **Tk 230–550m/yr (USD 1.9–4.5m/yr)** for the Savar estate alone, plus by-product revenue.
- **SOM [ASSUMPTION]:** the Savar contract plus 1–2 other industrial estates → Tk 400m–1bn/yr.

### 12. Economic / Social Impact
- **Export competitiveness:** the highest-ratio impact in the dossier — a small operating fix unlocking a multi-billion-dollar export sector, at exactly the moment LDC graduation makes non-RMG exports critical.
- **Employment:** leather and footwear are labour-intensive and employ many workers who would otherwise be RMG-dependent.
- **Environment and health:** the Dhaleshwari's contamination is a documented public health problem.

### 13. Technology Required
Physico-chemical and biological treatment, chrome recovery, sludge dewatering and management, continuous online effluent monitoring with public data feeds (the accountability mechanism), and flow equalisation sized for the Eid peak.

### 14. Government Role
- **Ministry of Industries**, BSCIC, Department of Environment, Export Promotion Bureau.
- **Government:** contract the operator on performance terms; enforce discharge standards and connection obligations; publish monitoring data.
- **Private:** operate the plant; provide compliance services.
- **PPP:** essential — the state has demonstrated it cannot operate this asset.
- **Policy change:** a tariff order allowing cost-recovery charging, and enforcement of source segregation obligations on tanneries.

### 15. Major Barriers
Tannery resistance to tariffs; the political sensitivity of enforcement against a sector employing many workers; capital for plant rehabilitation; and the credibility problem — a decade of failure has taught tanneries that non-compliance carries no consequence.

### 16. Difficulty of Implementation
**7/10.**

### 17. Opportunity Potential
**9/10** — one of the highest impact-per-taka opportunities identified.

### 18. Why Now?
LDC graduation on **24 November 2026** makes export diversification urgent; the government has an active plan for a new CETP (so capital is being mobilised anyway, and the contracting model can be fixed *before* it is spent); and EU due-diligence regulation is raising the compliance bar for leather imports, which widens the gap between certified and uncertified suppliers.

### 19. First 12-Month Pilot
- **Location:** Savar Tannery Industrial Estate.
- **Users:** 140 operating tanneries.
- **Partners:** Ministry of Industries/BSCIC, an international effluent operator, the tannery association, LWG, a DFI, and a brand buyer group.
- **Budget [ASSUMPTION]:** Tk 40–90 crore for rehabilitation and one year of contracted operations (a new plant is far larger).
- **KPIs:** effluent parameters vs standard (published daily); % of tanneries connected and segregating at source; number of tanneries achieving LWG certification; leather export value and unit price.
- **Success:** sustained compliant discharge for six consecutive months and at least 15 additional tanneries entering LWG audit.

### 20. OceanBlu / Entrepreneur Opportunity
Operating the CETP requires a specialist partner — but there is a very good adjacent business: **compliance, traceability and certification services for the leather and footwear value chain**. Build first: a service that takes tanneries and footwear factories through LWG readiness — chemical management, chain-of-custody from slaughterhouse to finished leather, and audit preparation — priced per factory per year. This is a business today, needs no plant, and every certified factory increases the client's export price. First customers: the larger Savar tanneries and export-oriented footwear manufacturers. Moat: audit relationships, accumulated remediation know-how, and traceability data. Scale: leather → footwear → textiles → become the local partner of choice for international certification bodies, and export the service to other South Asian leather clusters.
